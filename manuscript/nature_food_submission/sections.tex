\section*{Introduction}

Nutrient profiling systems convert food composition into food-level judgements
that can be used in dietary guidance, labelling, policy analysis and food-system
monitoring \cite{mozaffarian2021foodcompass,barrett2024foodcompass2}. Their
public-health value depends on a shared population-level ranking: a score must
remain stable enough to communicate which foods should generally be encouraged,
moderated or limited. Precision nutrition creates a different demand. Controlled
and free-living studies have shown that people can vary substantially in
postprandial glucose, lipid and inflammatory responses to the same foods
\cite{zeevi2015personalized,berry2020postprandial}. The central challenge is
therefore not only to personalize dietary advice, but to do so without erasing
the population consensus that makes nutrient profiling useful.

Most nutrient profiling systems were not designed to represent this
individual-food heterogeneity. A static score can rank foods for a population,
but the same value is assigned to every person. Fully personalized
recommendation models address the opposite problem, but they can become
difficult to interpret as nutrient profiles because the food-level prior is no
longer visible. A precision-ready nutrient profiling system should therefore
separate two functions: a universal food-quality prior that preserves dietary
guidance, and a bounded calibration term that expresses individual variation
around that prior.

We call this structure personalized calibration. In this formulation, each food
retains a baseline nutrient-profiling score, and each individual receives a
food-specific deviation constrained within a pre-specified range. The deviation
is centered at the food level, so the population mean remains anchored to the
baseline score. This design treats personalization as a calibration layer
rather than as a replacement for nutrient profiling. The key test is whether
such a system can preserve the food-level ranking while revealing heterogeneity
that a static score cannot express.

GMNPS implements personalized calibration using gut microbiome-informed
nutrient responses as a proof-of-concept individual layer. The gut microbiome is
a plausible calibration signal because it links habitual diet, microbial
metabolism and host metabolic traits, and large PREDICT/ZOE studies have mapped
microbiome associations with diet quality, cardiometabolic markers and
postprandial response variation
\cite{berry2020postprandial,asnicar2021microbiome,asnicar2025gut}. We use this
evidence to motivate a biologically interpretable calibration layer. We do not
claim that microbial features alone determine individual dietary response, and
we do not use the microbiome layer as a clinical decision model.

Here we test whether nutrient profiling can be extended for precision nutrition
through bounded personalized calibration. Food Compass 2.0 was used as the
selected baseline prior because it offers broad, current food-attribute coverage
across NHANES/FNDDS foods \cite{barrett2024foodcompass2}. GMNPS was evaluated
for population-level NPS preservation, individual heterogeneity, expert-reviewed
mask content validity, external cardiometabolic plausibility and known-ground
truth behaviour in a digital-gut-twin benchmark. The resulting claim is
deliberately bounded: GMNPS demonstrates computational feasibility and
biological plausibility for precision-ready nutrient profiling, while clinical
efficacy and individual-level human response prediction require further
retrospective or prospective validation.

\section*{Results}

\subsection*{GMNPS formulates precision nutrition as anchored calibration}

GMNPS begins with a food-level nutrient profiling system (NPS) prior and adds a
bounded individual-food deviation. For individual \(i\) and food \(j\), the
primary score is
\[
  \mathrm{GMNPS}_{ij} =
  \mathrm{clip}_{1,100}\left(\mathrm{NPS}_{j}+D_{ij}\right),
\]
where \(D_{ij}\) is the personalized calibration term. In the primary analysis,
\(D_{ij}\) was capped at 12 points. This cap allows visible individual
variation while limiting the ability of the personalized layer to overwhelm the
population prior.

The calibration term was designed to be interpretable. A raw personalized
nutrient response was decomposed into two pre-specified reporting channels: a
microbiota-accessible carbohydrate, short-chain-fatty-acid and plant-matrix
channel (MAC), and a lipid, bile-acid and trimethylamine-N-oxide-related channel
(LIPID). A residual component was retained for allocation but was not used as a
primary biological claim. These channels are modelling constraints for
interpretability; they are not evidence that the same mechanism has been
directly estimated from human intervention outcomes. Figure~\ref{fig:framework}
summarizes the score architecture, bounded transform and expert-revised channel
masks.

\subsection*{Population-level nutrient-profiling consensus is preserved}

We next tested whether personalized calibration preserved the population-level
NPS prior across the full v4 scoring run. The analysis included 9,234 foods for
which the baseline prior, nutrient vectors and microbiome-derived individual
weights could be aligned. The population-mean GMNPS score was nearly identical
to the baseline prior, with a Spearman correlation of 0.999839 between baseline
NPS and population-mean GMNPS (Fig.~\ref{fig:preservation}a). This result passed
the pre-specified preservation criterion of Spearman correlation at least 0.90.

Broad category transitions were rare at the population mean. Of the 9,234 foods,
79 shifted category after calibration (0.856\%). No food moved directly between
the minimize and encourage categories. The observed transitions were restricted
to adjacent boundaries: 38 foods moved from moderate to minimize, and 41 moved
from encourage to moderate (Fig.~\ref{fig:preservation}b). Food-group means also
remained closely aligned between the baseline prior and GMNPS population mean
(Fig.~\ref{fig:preservation}c). These results show that personalization did not
re-rank foods at the population level.

The expert-revised v4 mask improved anchoring relative to the previous aggregate
mask output. In the old v3 output, the Spearman correlation between the baseline
prior and population-mean score was 0.808736, with a mean total variance of
417.38. In the expert-revised v4 output, the corresponding correlation was
0.999839, and mean personalized delta variance was 26.27
(Fig.~\ref{fig:preservation}d). The comparison supports the decision to use the
expert-revised v4 mask as the primary analysis and to treat the old aggregate
mask as a historical sensitivity design.

\subsection*{GMNPS expresses bounded and channel-attributed heterogeneity}

Static nutrient profiling assigns no individual variance to a food. GMNPS
instead produced bounded individual-food deviations around the preserved
population mean. Mean delta variance was broadly comparable across food groups
because the primary transform capped and centered deviations, but channel
attributions differed by group (Fig.~\ref{fig:heterogeneity}a,b). Fruit and
vegetables were MAC-dominant, with mean MAC variances of 4.654 and 5.057,
respectively. Dairy, fats/oils and savory/sweet foods were LIPID-dominant, with
mean LIPID variances of 7.456, 6.495 and 8.126. Mixed dishes showed comparable
MAC and LIPID contributions (5.058 and 5.557), consistent with their
heterogeneous composition.

Food-level variance also separated MAC-dominant and LIPID-dominant regions of
the scoring space (Fig.~\ref{fig:heterogeneity}c). High-heterogeneity example
foods retained their baseline anchors while showing wide personalized
intervals across individuals (Fig.~\ref{fig:heterogeneity}d). These patterns
show that GMNPS can express individualized heterogeneity without turning the
score into an unanchored recommendation model. They should be interpreted as
model-attributed channel patterns, not as measured causal mechanisms in a human
intervention.

\subsection*{Expert review supports content validity of the calibration masks}

The primary masks were reviewed through a two-round structured expert
elicitation. Six experts with complementary backgrounds in clinical nutrition,
food analysis, food biomarkers, population nutrition, nutritional bioactive
compounds and probiotics reviewed the channel boundaries, food-group logic and
individual-output interpretability. The first round supported the food-group
consensus structure, and the second round supported the revised nutrient masks.
The review also identified clinical translation potential while emphasizing that
clinical application would require additional safety, disease-stage and
implementation constraints.

The expert review directly affected the primary analysis. Carbohydrate was
downgraded from a primary MAC variable to sensitivity or proxy status. Zinc and
copper were removed from the primary MAC channel. Vitamin A RAE was removed
from the LIPID channel, and retinol was retained as the lipid-channel vitamin A
marker. These revisions were implemented before the v4 scoring run and used to
define the primary mask version. The expert review therefore supports content
validity and interpretability of the modelling boundary, not clinical efficacy.

\subsection*{External and simulated benchmarks support plausibility and boundary}

We evaluated the microbiome layer with an external plausibility analysis and a
known-ground-truth synthetic benchmark (Fig.~\ref{fig:validation}). In an
independent GMrepo validation set \cite{dai2022gmrepo}, the microbiome-derived health-state component
showed modest discrimination for cardiometabolic phenotypes. The AUROC was
0.6332 for type 2 diabetes (95\% CI, 0.6115 to 0.6528; \(n=771\) disease
samples) and 0.6686 for coronary artery disease (95\% CI, 0.5823 to 0.7516;
\(n=47\) disease samples). The coronary artery disease result should be read
with caution because of the small disease sample size. Colorectal cancer showed
only weak discrimination, and inflammatory bowel phenotypes did not show a
positive signal. This pattern supports cardiometabolic plausibility but argues
against describing GMNPS as a general health predictor.

The digital-gut-twin benchmark tested the score architecture under known ground
truth. The FCS2-only baseline preserved the population ranking, as intended, but
its personalized residual recovery was not estimable because it had no
individualized deviation. Unanchored microbiome scoring recovered some
personalized residual structure (Spearman \(=0.247912\)) but reduced NPS
preservation to 0.932292. Anchored GMNPS recovered more personalized residual
structure (Spearman \(=0.395680\)) while preserving the NPS ranking
(Spearman \(=0.999965\)). Random microbiome weights and shuffled microbiome
profiles reduced personalized residual recovery to 0.047131 and 0.008768,
respectively. Delta-cap sensitivity analyses at caps of 8, 12 and 15 points
showed stable preservation and residual recovery. Together, these results
support the central computational claim: anchoring allows the personalized
layer to add individual signal without sacrificing the population NPS prior.

\section*{Discussion}

GMNPS reframes precision nutrition as a calibration problem for nutrient
profiling. The main contribution is not a replacement for existing NPS methods
and not a clinical recommender. It is an anchored score architecture in which a
population-level food-quality prior remains visible, while individual
heterogeneity is added as a bounded and interpretable deviation. This structure
addresses a practical tension in precision nutrition: personalization should
adapt to individual traits without dissolving the shared guidance needed for
public-health communication.

The evidence supports this architecture in three ways. First, population-level
NPS consensus was preserved across 9,234 foods. The population-mean GMNPS score
remained almost perfectly concordant with the baseline prior, and fewer than
1\% of foods crossed a broad category boundary. Second, the calibrated score
expressed individual heterogeneity that a static NPS cannot represent, with
MAC and LIPID channels providing a transparent attribution structure. Third,
the digital-gut-twin benchmark showed why anchoring matters: an unanchored
microbiome score could recover some residual signal, but it damaged the
population ranking; anchored GMNPS recovered more residual structure while
preserving the prior.

The gut microbiome is an informative proof-of-concept layer because it occupies
the interface between diet, microbial metabolism and host traits. Prior
PREDICT/ZOE studies link microbiome features with habitual diet,
cardiometabolic markers and postprandial response variation
\cite{berry2020postprandial,asnicar2021microbiome,asnicar2025gut}. Our external
analysis was consistent with this cardiometabolic framing: GMrepo signals were
strongest for type 2 diabetes and coronary artery disease, while inflammatory
bowel phenotypes defined a boundary condition. This result is useful because it
shows where the current health-state component is plausible and where it should
not be generalized.

Several limitations are central to the interpretation. The present analyses do
not estimate causal effects of microbes, nutrients or foods on host outcomes.
The digital-gut-twin benchmark tests behaviour under known ground truth but
does not replace individual-level human validation. Public ZOE/PREDICT resources
support biological plausibility and future validation routes, but restricted
host-response, dietary and intervention variables are needed to test whether
GMNPS deviations predict real individual metabolic responses. Expert review
supports content validity and interpretability of the mask boundary, not
clinical efficacy. Clinical use would also require constraints for disease
stage, medication, renal function, allergies, tolerability, cultural diet,
accessibility and clinician oversight.

The broader implication is that nutrient profiling can become precision-ready
without abandoning its population role. A personalized NPS should keep the
baseline prior explicit, keep the deviation bounded, report interpretable
drivers and state the validation boundary. GMNPS provides one microbiome-informed
implementation of this design. Future versions could incorporate metabolomics,
continuous glucose monitoring, genetics or other host-response layers if those
signals are evaluated under the same preservation and calibration criteria.

\section*{Methods}

\subsection*{Study design}

This study developed and evaluated GMNPS as a computational nutrient-profiling
framework. The primary task was to test whether a personalized calibration term
could be added to a baseline NPS prior while preserving population-level food
ranking. No new clinical experiment, human participant recruitment or dietary
intervention was conducted. Evidence came from the baseline NPS prior,
cycle-aligned food nutrient vectors, microbiome-derived individual nutrient
weights, expert-reviewed nutrient masks, external microbiome-health
plausibility analyses and synthetic digital-gut-twin benchmarking.

\subsection*{Baseline NPS prior and food universe}

Food Compass 2.0 was used as the selected baseline NPS prior
\cite{barrett2024foodcompass2}. It supplied the food-level score \(\mathrm{NPS}_j\)
on the 1 to 100 scale and was not treated as the main contribution of this
study. The primary scoring run used the aligned food universe available for the
v4 analysis, comprising 9,234 foods with baseline scores, food metadata and
nutrient vectors. Food Compass 2.0 was selected because it provides broad and
current attribute coverage across foods and beverages, making it suitable as an
anchoring prior for a proof-of-concept personalized NPS.

Food nutrient vectors were aligned to the Food and Nutrient Database for Dietary
Studies (FNDDS) context used by the baseline prior. Food identifiers were treated
as food records rather than as universal nutrient entities, and food metadata
were retained during matching. Ambiguous duplicated food-code records and
missing external category labels were handled as provenance issues for keyed
joins rather than as biological exclusions.

\subsection*{Microbiome-derived individual nutrient weights}

Each individual was represented by a nutrient weight vector \(\beta_i\) derived
from microbiome-associated nutrient response features. Each food was represented
by a standardized nutrient vector \(X_j\). Nutrient variables were preprocessed
with log transformation for heavy-tailed non-negative columns, percentile
winsorization and robust scaling with bounded clipping. The raw individual-food
response was computed as
\[
  R_{ij}=\beta_i X_j.
\]
This response is a computational calibration signal. It should not be
interpreted as a measured causal effect of a food, nutrient or microbial taxon
on a host outcome.

\subsection*{Bounded personalized calibration}

For each food, raw responses were robustly standardized across individuals to
obtain \(Z_{ij}\). The primary deviation was computed as
\[
  D_{ij}=12\tanh(Z_{ij}/2),
\]
centered within each food and clipped to the range \([-12,12]\). The final
score was then computed as
\[
  \mathrm{GMNPS}_{ij}=\mathrm{clip}_{1,100}(\mathrm{NPS}_j+D_{ij}).
\]
Sensitivity analyses used deviation caps of 8 and 15 points. The centering step
was included to preserve the population-level prior, and clipping was included
to keep the score on the 1 to 100 NPS scale.

\subsection*{Expert-revised channel masks}

The primary channel definition was the expert-revised v4 dual-channel mask. The
MAC channel included dietary fibre, alpha-carotene, beta-carotene,
beta-cryptoxanthin, lycopene, lutein plus zeaxanthin, vitamin C, food folate,
magnesium, potassium, phylloquinone and vitamin E. The LIPID channel included
total fat, total saturated fat, cholesterol, retinol, saturated fatty acids
C4:0, C6:0, C8:0, C10:0, C12:0, C14:0, C16:0 and C18:0, choline and vitamin
B12. Carbohydrate was reserved for sensitivity or proxy use. Zinc, copper and
vitamin A RAE were excluded from the primary microbiome channels, and retinol
was retained as the lipid-channel vitamin A marker.

The masks were reviewed through a two-round structured expert elicitation. Six
experts with backgrounds in clinical nutrition, food analysis, dietary
biomarkers, population nutrition, nutritional bioactive compounds and probiotics
reviewed channel boundaries, food-group logic and individual-output
interpretability. Expert review was used to support content validity and
interpretability, not to tune model performance after outcome inspection.

\subsection*{NPS preservation and heterogeneity analyses}

NPS preservation was evaluated by correlating the baseline NPS prior with the
population-mean GMNPS score across foods. The primary acceptance criterion was
Spearman correlation at least 0.90. Broad category transitions were counted
using three categories: minimize, moderate and encourage. Personalized
heterogeneity was summarized by food-level variance of \(D_{ij}\), MAC-attributed
variance and LIPID-attributed variance. Food-group summaries reported the number
of foods, mean baseline score, mean population GMNPS score, mean delta variance
and dominant channel.

The old aggregate mask output was compared with the expert-revised v4 output as
a design audit. Because the old output was already aggregated across nutrients,
it could not be decomposed exactly into the revised v4 nutrient set. It was
therefore treated as a historical sensitivity comparison rather than as the
primary analysis.

\subsection*{Digital-gut-twin benchmark}

The synthetic benchmark generated individual MAC and LIPID capacities, food
substrate exposures, a universal food-quality prior and a known host-response
matrix. Ground truth combined universal food quality, microbiome-conditioned
MAC and LIPID effects and random noise. The article run used 240 simulated
individuals, 120 simulated foods and random seed 42.

We compared FCS2-only scoring, unanchored microbiome scoring, anchored GMNPS,
random microbiome weights, shuffled microbiome profiles, the original nutrient
mask and the expert-revised nutrient mask. Metrics included Spearman correlation
with the full simulated individual response, Spearman correlation with the
personalized residual and Spearman correlation between the baseline NPS prior
and the population-mean predicted score. Personalized residual correlation was
reported as not estimable when the predicted residual had no rank variation, as
in the FCS2-only baseline.

\subsection*{External microbiome plausibility}

External microbiome plausibility was assessed using independent microbiome-health
outputs from GMrepo-based validation and public ZOE/PREDICT resources
\cite{dai2022gmrepo}. GMrepo
AUROC analyses were used to test whether the microbiome-derived health-state
component showed signal for selected disease comparisons against healthy
controls. The ZOE 2025 public microbiome resources were used as directionality
anchors for microbiome-health and microbiome-diet associations
\cite{asnicar2025gut}. Restricted individual-level host-response, dietary and
intervention variables were not used in the current analysis.

\section*{Data availability}

Food Compass 2.0 scores are available from the Food Compass 2.0 article and
supplement \cite{barrett2024foodcompass2}. FNDDS files should be obtained from
USDA FoodData Central and USDA Food Surveys Research Group resources using the
cycles corresponding to the baseline food universe. Public PREDICT/ZOE
metagenomic resources and limited metadata are available through the accessions
and repositories reported in the ZOE 2025 data availability statement
\cite{asnicar2025gut}. Restricted individual-level clinical, dietary and
intervention data require approved access from ZOE. Source data for the figures
are generated by the analysis scripts and provided with the manuscript package.
