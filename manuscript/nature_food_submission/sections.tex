\section*{Introduction}

Nutrient profiling systems translate food composition into population-level
judgements of healthfulness. They are used to support dietary guidance,
front-of-package labelling, food policy and food-system monitoring, and their
public-health value depends on stable rankings that agree with established
nutrition knowledge \cite{mozaffarian2021foodcompass,barrett2024foodcompass2}.
Yet nutrition responses vary across individuals. Controlled feeding and
free-living cohort studies show that people can differ markedly in
postprandial glucose, lipid and inflammatory responses to the same foods
\cite{zeevi2015personalized,berry2020postprandial}. This creates a tension for
precision nutrition: the field needs individual adaptation, but public-health
guidance still needs a shared food-level prior.

Static nutrient profiles are not designed to express this individual
heterogeneity. A static score can rank foods at the population level, but the
same number is assigned to every individual. Fully personalized recommendation
models address the opposite side of the problem, but they can drift away from
the interpretability and normative structure that make nutrient profiling useful
for policy and communication. A precision-ready NPS would therefore benefit from
a structure that preserves a universal nutrition prior while adding an explicit,
bounded and interpretable individual calibration layer.

We propose personalized calibration as that structure. The framework represents
each food by a baseline NPS score and adds an individual-food deviation that is
constrained to a pre-specified range. The deviation is centered at the food
level, so the population mean remains anchored to the static prior. This design
does not replace the baseline NPS. It asks whether an NPS can keep its
population-level consensus while revealing individual heterogeneity that a
static score necessarily hides.

GMNPS implements this idea with gut microbiome-informed nutrient responses as a
proof-of-concept calibration layer. The gut microbiome links habitual diet,
microbial metabolism and host metabolic traits, and large PREDICT/ZOE studies
have mapped microbiome associations with diet quality, cardiometabolic markers
and postprandial responses
\cite{asnicar2021microbiome,asnicar2025gut}. We use these findings to motivate
the microbiome as a biologically plausible calibration signal, not to claim that
microbial features alone determine dietary response. The microbiome layer is
one test case for a broader NPS transformation that could also include
metabolomics, continuous glucose response, genetics or other host traits.

Here we test whether nutrient profiling can be personalized as a bounded
calibration problem rather than replaced by a fully individualized recommender.
We use Food Compass 2.0 as the selected universal prior because it provides
broad and current food-attribute coverage across 9,273 NHANES/FNDDS foods
\cite{barrett2024foodcompass2}. We then evaluate whether GMNPS preserves
food-level rankings, recovers known personalized residual structure in a
synthetic benchmark and defines the evidence needed for retrospective human
validation. Public and restricted PREDICT/ZOE resources are treated as
biological plausibility anchors and future validation routes, not as clinical
efficacy evidence in the current analysis.

\section*{Results}

\subsection*{GMNPS defines personalized calibration as an anchored NPS transform}

GMNPS starts from a static nutrient-profiling prior and adds a bounded
individual deviation. For individual \(i\) and food \(j\), the score is
\[
  \mathrm{GMNPS}_{ij} = \mathrm{clip}_{1,100}\left(\mathrm{NPS}_{j} + D_{ij}\right).
\]
The deviation \(D_{ij}\) is derived from a microbiome-informed nutrient response
and capped at 12 points in the primary analysis. This cap is large enough to
express individual heterogeneity but small enough to prevent the personalized
layer from overwhelming the population prior. The score therefore remains an
NPS: it ranks foods on the 1-100 scale while adding a transparent individual
calibration term (Fig.~1).

The deviation is designed to be interpretable rather than free-floating. We
pre-specified two response channels for the proof-of-concept implementation: a
MAC/SCFA/plant-matrix channel and a lipid/bile-acid/TMAO channel. A residual
channel is used for allocation but not foregrounded as a biological result.
These channels are modelling constraints used for interpretability, not
evidence that the same channel structure has been estimated from human outcome
data.

\subsection*{Food Compass 2.0 provides an audited population-level prior}

We extracted Food Compass 2.0 Supplementary Table S5 from the provided
supplementary PDF and audited the resulting machine-readable table. The parser
recovered 9,273 rows, matching the expected Food Compass 2.0 food universe, and
passed score-range and score-difference checks. The audit identified 9,251
unique Foodcode values, 22 duplicated Foodcode values and 24 missing
Nutri-Score labels in the extracted text. For primary join-ready analyses we
therefore excluded 44 duplicated Foodcode rows and 24 rows with missing
Nutri-Score labels, leaving 9,205 rows. These exclusions do not affect the use
of Food Compass 2.0 as the baseline prior, but they prevent ambiguous keyed
joins during nutrient-vector alignment (Fig.~2a).

Because Food Compass 2.0 Table S5 reflects foods consumed in NHANES 2001-2002
through 2017-2018, nutrient vectors must be aligned to cycle-specific FNDDS
releases over the same period. We therefore treat Foodcode as a cycle-specific
identifier and preserve description and release year during matching. Current
FoodData Central releases are useful for future extension, but they do not
replace historical FNDDS cycles for primary baseline matching.

\subsection*{Population-level NPS consensus is preserved after calibration}

Anchored calibration preserved the population-level NPS prior in the synthetic
article run. Across 120 simulated foods, the Spearman correlation between the
baseline NPS score and the population-mean GMNPS score was 0.999965. No
population-mean category shifts were observed. These results reflect the
centering and bounding design: GMNPS introduces individual deviations but forces
their population mean to remain close to zero for each food. The calibrated
score therefore keeps the food-level consensus while allowing individual
scores to vary around it (Fig.~2b,c).

This preservation result is essential for the paper's NPS claim. Without the
anchor, microbiome-derived scoring can capture individual signal but risks
damaging the population ranking. GMNPS avoids that trade-off by making the
static NPS the prior and the microbiome response the calibration layer.

\subsection*{Bounded calibration reveals channel-specific heterogeneity}

GMNPS created individual-food heterogeneity that a static NPS cannot express.
In the synthetic benchmark, GMNPS recovered the channel structure encoded in
the simulator. Plant foods showed MAC-dominant heterogeneity
\((20.889\) MAC variance versus \(0.761\) lipid variance), whereas animal foods
showed lipid-dominant heterogeneity \((22.769\) lipid variance versus \(0.618\)
MAC variance). Mixed foods showed balanced but slightly lipid-dominant
heterogeneity (Fig.~3).

These results should be interpreted as a controlled simulation test rather than
as a new claim about real foods. The synthetic world encodes MAC and lipid
capacities by design, so the relevant result is not the discovery of the
pattern itself. The relevant result is that GMNPS can recover and report a
known channel-attributed structure while keeping each food anchored to a
universal NPS prior. This directly tests the computational behaviour required
for interpretable personalized calibration.

\subsection*{Anchored GMNPS balances NPS preservation and personalized recovery}

The synthetic digital-gut-twin benchmark compared GMNPS with static,
unanchored, random and shuffled alternatives under known ground truth. The
FCS2-only baseline performed as intended for population ranking but, by design,
could not express within-food personalized residual variation. Its personalized
residual rank correlation was therefore not estimable, because the predicted
residual was constant across all individual-food pairs. Unanchored microbiome
scoring produced a personalized residual Spearman correlation of 0.247912 but
reduced NPS preservation to 0.932292. Anchored GMNPS achieved a personalized
residual Spearman correlation of 0.395680 while preserving the NPS ranking at
0.999965 (Fig.~4).

Negative controls supported the specificity of the personalized signal in the
simulator. Random microbiome weights produced a residual correlation of
0.047131, and shuffled microbiome profiles produced a residual correlation of
0.008768. Both were far below anchored GMNPS. These results support the
central computational claim: anchoring allows the model to recover simulated
individual response signal without sacrificing the population NPS prior.

Sensitivity analyses supported the bounded-deviation design. Delta caps of 8,
12 and 15 points produced similar individual-response recovery
(Spearman 0.936205, 0.940040 and 0.940512) and similar personalized residual
recovery (Spearman 0.396858, 0.395680 and 0.393889). NPS preservation remained
0.999965 across the tested caps. Removing the NPS anchor lowered preservation,
whereas random and shuffled microbiome controls reduced personalized residual
recovery. Removing centering had little performance effect in this simulation,
which indicates that centering acts primarily as a calibration constraint in
the present benchmark rather than as a source of apparent accuracy.

\subsection*{Retrospective microbiome evidence supports plausibility but not efficacy}

Public PREDICT/ZOE resources provide a biologically grounded external anchor
for future retrospective analyses. The Nature 2025 ZOE microbiome study makes
raw metagenomes, public taxonomic profiles and limited metadata available, and
publishes favourable and unfavourable microbiome rankings associated with
health and diet \cite{asnicar2025gut}. These resources can test whether
pre-specified microbiome feature directions used by GMNPS are directionally
consistent with independent microbiome-health rankings.

Restricted individual-level ZOE clinical, dietary and intervention variables
are not public. Access requires a reviewed proposal and data-sharing agreement.
No restricted individual-level ZOE host-response, dietary or intervention data
were used in the current benchmark. For this reason, the current results should
be described as computational feasibility and retrospective biological
plausibility, not as individual-level human validation. A direct human
validation would require individual-level diet, microbiome and host-response
data with leakage-safe training and evaluation.

\section*{Discussion}

GMNPS reframes precision nutrition as a calibration problem for nutrient
profiling. The main contribution is not another static NPS and not an
unanchored recommendation model. It is a score architecture in which a
universal nutrition prior is preserved and individual heterogeneity is added as
a bounded deviation. This structure lets personalized nutrition enter the NPS
space without abandoning population-level dietary guidance.

The results support three connected claims. First, the baseline prior can be
audited and protected: Food Compass 2.0 Table S5 was recovered at the expected
row count, and population-mean GMNPS remained almost perfectly concordant with
the baseline. Second, individualized deviations can be made interpretable: MAC
and lipid channels provide a biological description of why a score differs
between individuals. Third, anchoring improves the trade-off between prior
preservation and personalized signal recovery in a known-ground-truth
simulation. The static model preserved the prior but had no residual
personalization; the unanchored model captured some residual signal but damaged
the NPS ranking; GMNPS balanced both.

The gut microbiome is a useful proof-of-concept layer because it sits at the
interface of diet, microbial metabolism and host traits. PREDICT/ZOE studies
show that the microbiome associates with habitual diet, cardiometabolic markers
and postprandial response variation
\cite{berry2020postprandial,asnicar2021microbiome,asnicar2025gut}. GMNPS uses
this literature to define a plausible calibration layer. The present analyses
do not estimate causal effects of microbes, nutrients or foods on host
outcomes. They instead define a constrained scoring architecture and test
whether its computational behaviour matches the intended calibration
properties. Future implementations could incorporate other individual layers if
they remain anchored, bounded and interpretable.

Several limitations define the current evidence boundary. The synthetic
benchmark is a known-ground-truth stress test, not a clinical validation. The
Food Compass 2.0 extraction is audited from a PDF supplement, but final
manuscript-grade FNDDS alignment should use official cycle-specific data and
resolve duplicated food codes before keyed joins. Public ZOE resources support
external biological plausibility, but restricted host-response variables are
needed to test whether GMNPS deviations predict real individual metabolic
responses. These limitations narrow the present claim to computational
feasibility for microbiome-informed NPS transformation.

The broader implication is that precision nutrition does not require nutrient
profiling to abandon shared dietary guidance. A personalized NPS can be built
as a calibrated score, where the population prior remains visible and the
individual deviation is explicitly bounded. This design gives future systems a
clear standard: personalization should add biologically interpretable
heterogeneity while preserving the nutrition consensus that makes NPS useful.

\section*{Methods}

\subsection*{Baseline NPS prior}

Food Compass 2.0 was used as the selected baseline NPS prior
\cite{barrett2024foodcompass2}. Food Compass 2.0 was chosen because it provides
broad and current NPS attribute coverage across foods and beverages. It was not
treated as the main contribution. The score supplied the population-level
baseline around which GMNPS deviations were centered.

Food Compass 2.0 Supplementary Table S5 was extracted from the user-provided
supplementary PDF using a conservative parser. The parser identifies table rows,
repairs wrapped food descriptions, stores Foodcode as a string and writes an
audit file containing row counts, duplicate Foodcode values, missing labels,
score range checks and malformed food-code rows. Raw PDFs, extraction scratch
files and generated output tables are excluded from Git.

\subsection*{FNDDS version alignment}

Food Compass 2.0 Table S5 describes foods consumed in NHANES 2001-2002 through
2017-2018 and contained in FNDDS 2001-2002 through 2017-2018. Nutrient vectors
should therefore be built from the same cycle-specific FNDDS releases. We
preserve Foodcode, normalized Description and FNDDS cycle during matching and
treat Foodcode as a cycle-specific identifier. If a duplicated Foodcode lacks an
explicit cycle in Table S5, the duplicate rows are kept as distinct food records
until an official spreadsheet or manual provenance review resolves the mapping.

\subsection*{Personalized deviation}

Let \(\beta_i\) be the microbiome-derived nutrient weight vector for individual
\(i\), and let \(X_j\) be the standardized nutrient vector for food \(j\). The
raw personalized response is
\[
  R_{ij} = \beta_i X_j.
\]
The response is decomposed into MAC/SCFA/plant-matrix, lipid/bile-acid/TMAO and
residual channels. The total response is robustly standardized across
individuals for each food, transformed with \(12 \tanh(Z_{ij}/2)\), centered by
food and clipped to the primary deviation cap. The final score is clipped to
the 1-100 range.

\subsection*{Expert-revised nutrient masks}

The primary MAC mask includes fibre, carotenoids, vitamin C, food folate,
magnesium, potassium, phylloquinone and vitamin E. Carbohydrate is reserved for
sensitivity analysis. Zinc and copper are excluded from the primary microbiome
channels. The lipid mask includes total fat, saturated fat, cholesterol,
retinol, selected saturated fatty acids, choline and vitamin B12. Vitamin A RAE
is excluded and retinol is retained.

\subsection*{Synthetic digital-gut-twin benchmark}

The synthetic benchmark generated individual microbial capacities for MAC and
lipid response, food substrate exposures, a universal food-quality prior and a
known host-response matrix. The ground truth combined universal food quality,
microbiome-conditioned MAC and lipid effects and random noise. We compared
FCS2-only scoring, unanchored microbiome scoring, anchored GMNPS, random
microbiome weights, shuffled microbiome profiles, the original nutrient mask
and the expert-revised mask.

The article run used 240 simulated individuals, 120 simulated foods and random
seed 42. The primary deviation cap was 12 points, with sensitivity analyses at
8 and 15 points.

Metrics included Spearman correlation with the full simulated individual
response, Spearman correlation with the personalized residual and Spearman
correlation between baseline NPS and population-mean predicted score.
Personalized residual rank correlation was reported as not estimable when the
predicted residual had no rank variance, as in the FCS2-only baseline.

\subsection*{External microbiome plausibility}

The published ZOE Microbiome Health Ranking 2025 was used as an external
biological plausibility anchor for pre-specified microbiome feature
directionality \cite{asnicar2025gut}. Public taxonomic profiles and limited
metadata can support retrospective plausibility analyses. Restricted
individual-level host, dietary and intervention variables require approved
access and were not used in the current synthetic benchmark.

\section*{Data availability}

Food Compass 2.0 scores are available from the Food Compass 2.0 article and
supplement \cite{barrett2024foodcompass2}. FNDDS files should be obtained from
the USDA FoodData Central and USDA ARS Food Surveys Research Group download
pages using the 2001-2002 through 2017-2018 cycles. Public PREDICT/ZOE
metagenomic resources and limited metadata are available through the accessions
and Zenodo records reported in the ZOE 2025 data availability statement
\cite{asnicar2025gut}. Restricted individual-level clinical, dietary and
intervention data require approved access from ZOE.
