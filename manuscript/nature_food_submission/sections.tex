\section*{Introduction}

Nutrient profiling systems translate food composition into common food-quality
scales for dietary guidance, labelling and policy comparison
\cite{mozaffarian2021foodcompass,barrett2024foodcompass2}. Their value comes
from a shared premise: the same food should have the same public-health meaning
when it is compared across products, food groups and populations. That premise
has become harder to reconcile with precision nutrition. Deep phenotyping and
challenge-meal studies show that glycaemic, lipaemic and other metabolic
responses vary substantially between people who consume the same foods
\cite{zeevi2015personalized,berry2020postprandial,bermingham2024personalized}.
A nutrient profile designed for precision use therefore has to adapt to the
person without losing the population-level ranking that makes the score
interpretable.

Current strategies tend to solve only one side of this problem. Static nutrient
profiles preserve a stable food ranking, but assign every person the same score.
Fully individualized recommendation models can represent response
heterogeneity, but their population food-quality prior may become implicit,
weakly constrained or difficult to audit. This distinction matters because a
system that freely re-ranks foods may cease to function as nutrient profiling.
The unresolved design challenge is to let personalization change the score
without letting it replace the food-quality prior.

We define this design as personalized calibration. A food retains an explicit
population nutrient-profile score, and an individual receives a bounded
person-food deviation around that anchor. The deviation is centred at the food
level, capped before scoring and returned to the same 1 to 100 scale. This
architecture makes the validation problem visible. At the population mean, the
calibrated score should preserve nutritional consensus. At the individual
level, it should expose structured heterogeneity that a static profile cannot
express.

The gut microbiome provides a biologically grounded test case for personalized
calibration, rather than a complete basis for clinical dietary decisions. Diet
rapidly affects microbiome composition \cite{david2014diet}, habitual diet is
associated with microbial community structure
\cite{wu2011enterotypes,zhernakova2016populationMetagenomics,
falony2016populationVariation,rothschild2018environment}, and
microbiota-targeted dietary interventions can modify immune and metabolic
readouts \cite{wastyk2021microbiotaDiets}. Microbial metabolism also links
dietary substrates with short-chain fatty acids, bile acids and trimethylamine
N-oxide-related pathways \cite{koh2016scfa,makki2018fiber,
wang2011phosphatidylcholine,koeth2013carnitine,wahlstrom2016bileacids}.
Population-scale PREDICT and ZOE studies further connect microbiome features
with habitual diet, cardiometabolic traits and postprandial responses
\cite{asnicar2021microbiome,asnicar2025gut,fackelmann2025diet,
segev2026dietmicrobiome}. Together, these data support the microbiome as a
plausible calibration layer, while leaving clinical efficacy to future
validation.

Here we introduce GMNPS as a microbiome-informed example of personalized
calibration for nutrient profiling. We use Food Compass 2.0 only as the
selected baseline prior because it provides broad current food-attribute
coverage \cite{barrett2024foodcompass2}. The study asks whether a bounded
calibration layer can preserve population nutrition consensus, reveal
model-attributed person-food heterogeneity, pass expert boundary review and
behave as expected in external plausibility and known-ground-truth simulation
analyses. The central claim is bounded: personalized calibration can make
nutrient profiling more suitable for precision nutrition, and a gut microbiome
layer offers a feasible demonstration, but prospective or deeply phenotyped
retrospective studies are still required before clinical use.

\section*{Results}

\subsection*{Bounded calibration anchored personalized scores to a population prior}

GMNPS converted personalization into a bounded calibration step around an
explicit population food-quality prior (Fig.~\ref{fig:framework}). For each
individual-food pair, microbiome-informed nutrient-response features generated
a raw deviation. This deviation was robustly standardized, mapped through a
bounded transform and added to the retained prior. The primary deviation cap
was 12 points on the 1 to 100 scale, so the final score remained a calibrated
nutrient profile rather than an unconstrained recommendation score.

The calibrated deviation was reported through two expert-revised channels that
separated plant-matrix and lipid-related food attributes. The plant-matrix
channel captured fibre, selected carotenoids, vitamin C, folate, magnesium,
potassium, phylloquinone and vitamin E. The lipid-related channel captured
fat, saturated fat, cholesterol, retinol, selected saturated fatty acids,
choline and vitamin B12. The channel labels were used to attribute modelled
variance; they were not treated as causal mechanism readouts from intervention
experiments.

\subsection*{Personalized calibration preserved population nutrition consensus}

Personalized calibration preserved the population food ranking across the
aligned food universe (Fig.~\ref{fig:preservation}). The primary run combined
baseline scores, nutrient vectors and microbiome-derived weights for 9,234
foods and 15,492 microbiome profiles. The population-mean GMNPS score was
almost identical to the baseline prior across foods (Spearman
\(\rho=0.999839\)), exceeding the pre-specified preservation criterion of
\(\rho \geq 0.90\).

Broad food-category assignments changed rarely after calibration at the
population mean. Only 63 of 9,234 foods crossed broad category boundaries
(0.68\%). All movements occurred between adjacent categories; no food moved
directly between the lowest and highest broad categories. Food-group means were
also nearly superimposable between the baseline prior and the calibrated
population mean. Bootstrap resampling showed that preservation was stable to
the composition of the scored food universe. These analyses do not establish
clinical validity. They establish that the calibrated score retained the
population-level behaviour expected of a nutrient profile.

\subsection*{Anchored scores revealed structured person-food heterogeneity}

Anchored calibration introduced bounded individual variation that a static
nutrient profile cannot express (Fig.~\ref{fig:heterogeneity}). Static baseline
scores have no individual variance by design. GMNPS preserved the food-level
anchor while allowing individual profiles to vary within the cap. A heat map of
high-variance profiles and representative foods showed that deviations were not
constant across people or foods, indicating that calibration added
person-specific structure rather than a uniform offset.

Channel attribution varied across food groups in patterns consistent with the
substrate logic of the calibration layer. Vegetables and legumes or nuts more
often showed plant-matrix dominance, whereas fruit, grains, dairy, fats and
oils, meat and egg products, seafood, and savoury or sweet foods showed
larger lipid-related contributions in the current scoring run. These patterns
should be read as model-attributed heterogeneity. They do not by themselves
show that the microbiome caused the predicted response for a specific person
and food.

\subsection*{Expert review sharpened the microbiome-channel boundary}

Structured expert review refined the biological boundary before the primary
scoring run was interpreted. Six experts with complementary backgrounds in
clinical nutrition, food analysis, food bioactives, food biomarkers, population
nutrition and probiotics completed two review rounds. The first round supported
the food-group and output logic. The second round supported the revised
nutrient-channel boundary after targeted changes. The review also identified
clinical translation potential while noting that clinical application would
require additional constraints for disease stage, medication use, safety and
implementation.

The review changed the primary nutrient-channel mask in ways that improved
specificity without tuning model performance. Carbohydrate was downgraded to a
sensitivity or proxy variable because it was too broad to represent
microbiota-accessible substrate on its own. Zinc and copper were removed from
the plant-matrix channel. Vitamin A RAE was removed from the lipid-related
channel, while retinol was retained as the more specific marker. The original
mask was therefore treated as a sensitivity analysis rather than the primary
boundary.

\subsection*{External and simulated benchmarks defined where calibration was plausible}

External microbiome-health comparisons supported a cardiometabolic
plausibility boundary rather than general disease prediction
(Fig.~\ref{fig:validation}). In GMrepo comparisons against healthy controls
\cite{dai2022gmrepo}, the microbiome-derived health-state component showed
modest discrimination for type 2 diabetes (area under the receiver operating
characteristic curve 0.6332; 95\% CI, 0.6115 to 0.6528; \(n=771\) disease
samples) and coronary artery disease (0.6686; 95\% CI, 0.5823 to 0.7516;
\(n=47\) disease samples). Signals were weak or negative for colorectal cancer
and inflammatory bowel disease comparisons. This analysis was used only to
bound plausibility; it did not validate individual food scores.

In known-ground-truth simulations, anchoring preserved the population prior
while retaining individualized residual structure. Across 12 repeated seeds,
the baseline-only model preserved the population ranking but had no defined
personalized residual because its prediction did not vary by individual.
Anchored GMNPS preserved the prior (mean \(\rho=0.9998\), s.d. 0.0001) and
recovered individualized residual structure (mean \(\rho=0.3771\), s.d.
0.0284). Random and shuffled microbiome controls retained the prior but lost
the residual signal (mean residual \(\rho=0.0137\) and 0.0102). The unanchored
microbiome score recovered some residual structure but weakened preservation of
the population prior. Varying the deviation cap from 8 to 15 points did not
materially change this trade-off. These results support the design claim that
bounded anchoring lets a personalized layer add individual signal without
collapsing the population food-quality prior.

\section*{Discussion}

This study reframes precision nutrition as a calibration problem for nutrient
profiling. The main contribution is not a clinical recommendation model, but an
auditable architecture in which a population food-quality prior remains
explicit and personalization enters as a bounded individual-food deviation.
This distinction preserves the public-health role of nutrient profiling while
creating a controlled space for person-specific biology.

The evidence supports this architecture at three levels. The population mean
preserved the baseline food ranking across more than nine thousand foods, so
GMNPS retained the behaviour expected of a nutrient profile. Individual scores
revealed bounded person-food heterogeneity, and channel attribution made that
heterogeneity interpretable without presenting it as causal proof. The
digital-gut-twin benchmark then showed why anchoring matters: an unanchored
microbiome score could capture individualized signal but weakened the
population prior, whereas anchored GMNPS balanced both objectives.

The microbiome layer should be interpreted as a proof of concept. Human studies
support the gut microbiome as a link between diet quality, microbial metabolism
and host metabolic traits \cite{sanz2025gutmicrobiome,asnicar2021microbiome,
manor2020healthMarkers,asnicar2025gut,segev2026dietmicrobiome}. The present
analysis uses that biology to define an interpretable calibration layer, but it
does not show that GMNPS predicts clinical outcomes for a given person. This
boundary is important because microbiome-diet associations are shaped by
habitual diet, medication, disease state, geography and measurement pipelines.
It also reflects broader concerns that nutrient-profiling systems and
prediction models require transparent validation before being used in decision
settings \cite{ortenzi2023foodCompassLimitations,vanCalster2019calibration,
collins2015tripod,wolff2019probast,collins2024tripodAi}.

More broadly, personalized calibration offers a modular path for
precision-ready nutrient profiling. The baseline prior could be replaced by
another validated nutrient profile, and the calibration layer could incorporate
microbiome, metabolomic, continuous-glucose, genetic or clinical signals if
each layer is evaluated under the same preservation, interpretability and
validation criteria. Future studies should test whether calibrated deviations
predict measured postprandial or longer-term responses in cohorts with matched
foods, microbiomes and host outcomes before the framework is used for clinical
decision-making.

\section*{Methods}

\subsection*{Study design}

This was a computational framework and validation study. No new human
participants were recruited and no clinical intervention was conducted. The
analysis combined a baseline nutrient-profiling prior, aligned food nutrient
vectors, microbiome-derived individual weights, expert-reviewed channel masks,
external microbiome-health plausibility analysis and digital-gut-twin
simulation.

\subsection*{Baseline prior and scored food universe}

Food Compass 2.0 was used as the selected baseline prior because it provides
broad food-attribute coverage on a 1 to 100 scale
\cite{barrett2024foodcompass2}. It was used as an anchoring prior rather than
as the focus of the contribution. The primary scoring run included 9,234
aligned foods with baseline scores, food metadata and nutrient vectors. Nutrient
files were aligned to the Food and Nutrient Database for Dietary Studies context
used by the baseline prior.

\subsection*{Personalized calibration score}

Each individual was represented by a microbiome-derived nutrient weight vector,
and each food was represented by a standardized nutrient vector. Individual-food
responses were computed as the product of these vectors, robustly standardized,
mapped through a bounded hyperbolic tangent transform and centred at the food
level. The centred deviation was capped at 12 points for the primary analysis
and added to the baseline prior; final scores were clipped to the 1 to 100
range. Sensitivity analyses used caps of 8 and 15 points. In this article,
microbiome-derived weights were treated as calibration features rather than
causal effect estimates.

\subsection*{Expert-reviewed channel masks}

The expert-revised primary mask defined two reporting channels. The
plant-matrix channel included fibre, selected carotenoids, vitamin C, food
folate, magnesium, potassium, phylloquinone and vitamin E. The lipid-related
channel included total fat, saturated fat, cholesterol, retinol, selected
saturated fatty acids, choline and vitamin B12. Carbohydrate was retained only
for sensitivity or proxy use, and zinc, copper and vitamin A RAE were excluded
from primary microbiome-channel attribution. Six experts reviewed the channel
boundary and output logic in two rounds before the primary analysis was
interpreted. The review was used for content validity and interpretability, not
for performance tuning.

\subsection*{Evaluation and benchmarking}

Population preservation was evaluated by Spearman correlation between the
baseline prior and the population-mean GMNPS score, with a pre-specified
criterion of \(\rho \geq 0.90\). Broad category shifts were counted at the
population mean. Heterogeneity was summarized by food-level variance,
individual-food intervals and channel-attributed variance. External
plausibility was assessed with GMrepo disease comparisons against healthy
controls \cite{dai2022gmrepo}. The digital-gut-twin benchmark generated
synthetic individual microbial capacities, food substrate exposures and known
host responses, following virtual-twin concepts in precision nutrition
\cite{adams2020digitaltwins}. It compared baseline-only scoring, unanchored
microbiome scoring, anchored GMNPS, random microbiome weights, shuffled
microbiome profiles and architecture ablations across repeated seeds.

\section*{Data availability}

Food Compass 2.0 scores are available from the original Food Compass 2.0
article and supplement \cite{barrett2024foodcompass2}. FNDDS files are
available from USDA FoodData Central and USDA Food Surveys Research Group
resources. Public PREDICT/ZOE microbiome resources are available through the
repositories reported in the 2025 ZOE data availability statement
\cite{asnicar2025gut}. Restricted individual-level clinical, dietary and
intervention variables require approved access from the data holders. Figure
source data are generated by the analysis scripts and included with the
manuscript package.
